Deadlines, Jitter, and Real-Time Constraints

Reason about when work must finish, how timing variation affects behavior, and how to measure deadline misses.

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish timing correctness from functional correctness.
  • Define latency, execution time, period, deadline, and jitter.
  • Measure deadline misses under representative load.

Correctness includes time

A real-time system must produce the right result within an acceptable time bound. A late actuator command may be wrong even if its numeric value is correct. Hard deadlines have severe consequences when missed; soft real-time systems may tolerate lateness with degraded quality.

Timing vocabulary

Execution time is how long work runs on the processor; response time includes waiting and interference. A period is the intended activation interval, while a deadline is the latest acceptable completion. Latency is the delay from event to response. State which one is being measured.

Jitter and distributions

Jitter is variation in activation or completion timing. Averages hide rare long delays, so record median, high percentiles, maximum, and deadline misses. Instrumentation itself can add overhead; measure consistently.

Budget and overload

Account for interrupts, critical sections, bus waits, logging, and other tasks. Decide how overload is handled: coalesce updates, drop stale samples, reduce optional work, or enter a fault state. Do not infer a guarantee from a loop that is usually fast.

Practical Lab

  1. State a periodic activity's period and deadline in milliseconds.
  2. Timestamp activation and completion with monotonic time for at least 100 runs.
  3. Calculate maximum response time and jitter; report deadline misses, not only average.
  4. Add controlled workload and document overload handling.

Common Pitfall

Do not use average loop speed as proof of a real-time guarantee; rare long delays often determine deadline success.

Check Your Understanding

1. Is a fast average enough to prove a deadline?

No. Response-time bounds and deadline misses matter.

2. What is jitter?

Variation in timing, such as activation or completion time.

3. What should happen under overload?

Apply a defined policy such as coalescing, dropping stale data, reducing optional work, or entering a fault state.

Key Takeaways

  • Distinguish timing correctness from functional correctness.
  • Define latency, execution time, period, deadline, and jitter.
  • Measure deadline misses under representative load.
