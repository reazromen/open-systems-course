Voltage, Current, Resistance, and Power

Build the electrical model needed to connect components safely and reason about what a circuit is doing.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish voltage, current, resistance, and power.
  • Use Ohm’s law to estimate current and resistor values.
  • Explain why a GPIO is a signal source, not a general-purpose power supply.

Four quantities that explain many beginner circuits

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points. Current is the rate at which charge flows. Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current in a simple resistive model. Power is the rate of energy transfer. These quantities are related, but they are not interchangeable.

For a resistor, Ohm’s law gives V = I × R. Rearranging gives I = V / R and R = V / I. The power dissipated by a resistive load can be calculated with P = V × I, or with equivalent resistor forms P = I²R and P = V²/R.

Worked example: an indicator LED

Suppose a 3.3 V GPIO drives a red LED with an approximate forward voltage of 2.0 V through a 330 Ω resistor. A first-order estimate is:

Voltage across resistor = 3.3 V − 2.0 V = 1.3 V
Estimated current = 1.3 V / 330 Ω ≈ 0.00394 A ≈ 3.9 mA

This is an estimate, not a universal design prescription. LED forward voltage varies, GPIO drive limits depend on the MCU and operating conditions, and board-level circuits may add constraints. Check the relevant datasheets and select a safe current with margin.

Why current matters

A 3.3 V logic level does not mean a pin can supply arbitrary current. A GPIO has output-current limits and voltage behavior that changes with load. Motors, relay coils, speakers, and other loads may require much more current or generate voltage spikes. Use a suitable transistor, MOSFET, driver IC, flyback protection, or dedicated power stage where required.

Common reference: ground

Voltage is measured between two points. When we say a signal is 3.3 V, we usually mean 3.3 V relative to the circuit's ground reference. Two modules communicating electrically normally need a compatible signal reference as well as compatible signal levels. Grounding and power distribution must be designed deliberately, especially when high-current loads share a supply.

Practical Lab

  1. Draw a 3.3 V supply, resistor, and LED in series, with a return path to ground.
  2. Estimate current for 220 Ω, 330 Ω, and 1 kΩ using an assumed 2.0 V LED drop.
  3. Calculate the resistor's approximate power dissipation for the 330 Ω case.
  4. Compare the result with the resistor rating and the MCU's GPIO documentation.
  5. Write down which assumptions would need checking on the real board.

Expected result: Three resistor/current estimates with units and a circuit diagram showing the LED, current-limiting resistor, supply, and return path.

Troubleshooting: Recheck LED forward voltage and resistor power assumptions; disconnect power before changing wiring and never use a GPIO as a load supply.

Common Pitfall

Treating a pin's logic voltage as proof that it can power a load. Voltage compatibility, current capability, power dissipation, and transient protection are separate questions.

Check Your Understanding

1. What does 3.3 V tell you about GPIO current?

It describes a voltage level relative to a reference; it does not say how much current the pin can safely source or sink.

2. Why is the LED resistor calculation an estimate?

The LED forward voltage and GPIO output voltage vary, and the circuit must respect component and MCU limits.

3. Why should a motor not be driven directly from a GPIO?

Its current demand and inductive transients can exceed the pin's capability. Use an appropriate driver and protection circuit.

Key Takeaways

  • Distinguish voltage, current, resistance, and power.
  • Use Ohm’s law to estimate current and resistor values.
  • Explain why a GPIO is a signal source, not a general-purpose power supply.
