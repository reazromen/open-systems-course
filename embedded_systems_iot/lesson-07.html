Logic Levels, Pull-Ups, and Open-Drain Signals

Understand how digital inputs become stable electrical signals and why many buses use pull-up resistors.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish logic thresholds from exact ideal voltages.
  • Explain floating inputs and the role of pull-up and pull-down resistors.
  • Describe open-drain signaling and why I²C commonly uses pull-ups.

Digital does not mean electrically exact

A digital input interprets a voltage as a logical low or high according to specified input thresholds. The thresholds are ranges, not a promise that every low is exactly 0.000 V or every high is exactly 3.300 V. Noise margin matters: a signal should stay safely inside the receiver's valid range under expected load, cable length, and interference.

What happens when an input is floating?

A high-impedance input that is not connected to a defined signal may pick up noise and appear to switch unpredictably. A pull-up weakly connects the input to the logic supply; a pull-down weakly connects it to ground. A button can then connect the input to the opposite level when pressed. The exact active level depends on the wiring and firmware configuration.

Many microcontrollers provide configurable internal pull resistors, but their resistance range and availability vary. External resistors may be needed for a known value, noisy environments, long wires, or bus timing.

Open-drain outputs

An open-drain output can pull a line low or release it; it does not actively drive the line high. A pull-up resistor raises the shared line when every participant releases it. Multiple devices can therefore share a line without one device driving high while another drives low. I²C uses this arrangement for its SDA and SCL signals.

The pull-up value is a trade-off. A stronger pull-up improves rise time but increases current while a device holds the line low. A weaker pull-up reduces low-state current but can make transitions too slow, especially with bus capacitance. Use the bus specification, device limits, voltage, wiring, and bus speed to select a valid value.

Voltage compatibility is mandatory

Do not assume a module is safe because its power connector accepts 5 V. Its signal pins may still use 3.3 V logic. Pulling a shared line up to 5 V can damage a non-5-V-tolerant MCU even if the module itself has a regulator. Verify both devices and any level-shifting circuit.

Practical Lab

  1. Draw an active-low button with a pull-up and label the input voltage in pressed and released states.
  2. Explain what could happen if the pull-up is omitted and the input is left disconnected.
  3. Look up the I²C sensor and MCU logic-voltage requirements for your chosen board.
  4. Record where the bus pull-ups come from and which voltage they connect to. Do not power the circuit until this is verified.

Expected result: A truth table for pressed/released states and a diagram showing the pull resistor and expected GPIO voltage.

Common Pitfall

Assuming a digital input always reads a stable 0 or 1, or assuming I²C pull-ups can connect to any convenient supply. Floating inputs and mismatched pull-up voltage cause real faults.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why does a floating GPIO produce unreliable readings?

Its voltage is not held within a defined logic range and can be influenced by noise and leakage.

2. What does an open-drain output do when it is not pulling low?

It releases the line; a pull-up resistor or another permitted circuit establishes the high level.

3. Why can a 5 V I²C pull-up be dangerous for a 3.3 V MCU?

The line may exceed the MCU input's allowed voltage unless the device explicitly tolerates it or proper level shifting is used.
