Requirements, Constraints, and Trade-offs

Turn a vague product idea into requirements that can guide hardware, firmware, and test design.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Separate functional requirements from quality attributes and constraints.
  • Write at least three measurable requirements for a small IoT device.
  • Explain one trade-off involving latency, power, cost, or reliability.

From an idea to something testable

“Build a smart plant monitor” is a useful idea, but it is not yet an engineering specification. How often must the device measure soil moisture? Is a reading required if Wi-Fi is unavailable? Should it operate on batteries? What happens when a sensor disconnects? How much delay is acceptable between a measurement and an alert? Different answers produce different hardware, firmware, and cloud designs.

A requirement says what the system must do or what condition it must satisfy. A good requirement is clear enough that another person can test whether it was met. “Use little power” is vague. “In the defined sampling and reporting profile, average current must remain below the measured design target” is testable once the profile and target are specified.

Three categories

  • Functional requirements: what the system does, such as read a sensor, show a warning, or publish telemetry.
  • Quality attributes: how well it must operate, such as response time, availability, energy use, accuracy, or maintainability.
  • Constraints: boundaries the design must respect, such as board choice, pin count, enclosure size, budget, operating temperature, certification, and development time.

Safety requirements deserve special attention. They may specify the safe state after a fault, limits on an actuator, or a rule that an output must never energize during boot. A behavior that is “usually correct” may be unacceptable when a physical output can injure someone or damage equipment.

Example: a room-temperature monitor

The numbers above are illustrative, not universal targets. A real product needs a usage profile, test conditions, and an agreed tolerance before these become acceptance criteria.

Trade-offs are design decisions

Reporting every second may make a dashboard feel responsive, but it can increase radio activity and energy use. Sampling more often may capture short events, but it increases processing and storage costs. A cheaper sensor can reduce bill-of-materials cost while requiring more calibration. Storing data locally improves outage tolerance but consumes flash and introduces retention and wear questions.

The correct answer depends on requirements. Good engineering records the reason for a choice and what evidence would cause the team to revisit it.

Practical Lab

Write a one-page mini-specification for a plant-moisture monitor or room-temperature monitor. Include at least three functional requirements, two measurable quality attributes, three constraints, one safe-state rule, and a test method for each requirement. Mark each value as assumption until it is confirmed.

Expected result: A mini-specification with at least three measurable functional requirements and one explicit constraint or trade-off.

Troubleshooting: Rewrite words such as “fast,” “reliable,” or “low power” using units, limits, operating conditions, and a testable pass criterion.

Common Pitfall

Writing requirements that cannot be tested, or choosing a board and framework before the product requirements are known. Record assumptions and acceptance tests before committing to an implementation.

Check Your Understanding

1. What is the difference between a functional requirement and a quality attribute?

A functional requirement states what the system must do. A quality attribute constrains how well it must do it, for example latency, power, reliability, or accuracy.

2. Why is “low power” not yet an acceptance criterion?

It lacks a defined workload, measurement method, and target. Different duty cycles can produce very different current measurements.

3. What is a design trade-off?

A decision that improves one property while affecting another, such as reporting faster at the cost of battery life.

Key Takeaways

  • Separate functional requirements from quality attributes and constraints.
  • Write at least three measurable requirements for a small IoT device.
  • Explain one trade-off involving latency, power, cost, or reliability.
