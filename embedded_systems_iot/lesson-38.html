Queues, Mutexes, Semaphores, and Notifications

Choose synchronization primitives based on whether you need data transfer, mutual exclusion, resource counting, or a lightweight signal.

Learning Objectives

  • Select a primitive based on the synchronization problem.
  • Distinguish mutex ownership from semaphore signaling.
  • Define timeout, ordering, and overflow behavior.

Queues transfer data

A queue carries values or records between producers and consumers. Decide whether it copies data or passes pointers, what order is guaranteed, how full conditions are handled, and whether stale samples should be dropped or preserved.

Mutexes protect shared resources

A mutex provides mutual exclusion with ownership semantics; the task that locks it is responsible for unlocking it. Keep critical sections short and avoid holding a mutex across slow I/O. Recursive mutexes and priority inheritance are platform-specific.

Semaphores signal or count

A binary semaphore can signal an event; a counting semaphore can represent available resources or pending events. A semaphore generally does not express exclusive ownership like a mutex. Task notifications can be efficient for simple one-to-one signaling on some RTOSes.

Deadlocks and timeouts

Deadlock can arise when tasks wait cyclically for resources. Define a consistent lock order, minimize nested locks, and use timeouts with meaningful recovery behavior. A timeout does not replace correct ownership; log enough context to diagnose failures.

Practical Lab

  1. Choose a primitive for sensor transfer, protecting a configuration structure, signaling DMA completion, and counting available buffers.
  2. Draw producer/consumer ownership for a queue carrying pointers.
  3. Draw a two-resource lock cycle and propose a lock-order rule.
  4. Specify behavior when a queue is full or a wait times out.

Common Pitfall

Do not use a mutex as an event queue or hold locks across slow I/O; choose primitives by their semantics.

Check Your Understanding

1. What is a queue for?

Transferring data with defined capacity and ordering.

2. What distinguishes a mutex?

Mutual exclusion with ownership semantics.

3. How can lock-order rules help?

They reduce cyclic waiting patterns that can cause deadlock.

Key Takeaways

  • Select a primitive based on the synchronization problem.
  • Distinguish mutex ownership from semaphore signaling.
  • Define timeout, ordering, and overflow behavior.
