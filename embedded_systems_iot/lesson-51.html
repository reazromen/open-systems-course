Threat Modeling and Device Identity

Map assets, trust boundaries, attackers, and device identity before selecting security controls.

Learning Objectives

  • Identify assets and attack surfaces.
  • Draw trust boundaries.
  • Plan unique device identity.

Identify assets and trust boundaries

List what needs protection: device identity, firmware integrity, credentials, telemetry, control commands, and service availability. Draw the device, phone or gateway, network, broker/API, and update service as separate trust zones. Mark every boundary across which data or authority moves.

Threats and attack paths

For each asset, ask who could attack it, what access they have, and what could happen if a control fails. Consider physical access, credential theft, replayed commands, malicious updates, exposed debug interfaces, and service compromise. Prioritize plausible impact rather than making an unbounded list of hypothetical threats.

Device identity

Give devices unique identities when per-device authorization, revocation, and auditing are required. Shared fleet credentials make one compromise affect many devices. Define enrollment, credential storage, rotation, revocation, and end-of-life handling.

Mitigations and residual risk

Map each high-priority threat to a control and a test that provides evidence the control works. Record remaining risk and assumptions. Threat modeling is a living engineering artifact: update it when interfaces, deployment, or attacker capabilities change.

Practical Lab

  1. Draw trust boundaries; list assets and attacker actions; rank top risks and map each to a concrete mitigation and verification test.

Expected result: A prioritized threat register linking assets and attack paths to mitigations, verification tests, and residual risk.

Troubleshooting: Review each risk against a specific trust boundary and realistic capability; do not mark a mitigation complete without a testable acceptance criterion.

Common Pitfall

Writing a threat model without assigning mitigations and verification tests.

Check Your Understanding

1. What is a trust boundary?

A point where data or authority crosses between different trust assumptions.

2. What follows each identified risk?

A prioritized mitigation and a verification test.

3. Why avoid fleet-wide shared secrets?

One compromise could affect many devices and complicate revocation.

Key Takeaways

  • Model assets, attackers, trust boundaries, and plausible attack paths.
  • Use unique device identities when per-device authorization is needed.
  • Link mitigations to tests and document residual risk.
