Embedded Systems & IoT · Module 1 · Lesson 03

Microcontroller, Microprocessor, and SoC

Compare common computing platforms and choose one from measurable product needs.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Describe the role of a CPU, MCU, MPU, memory, and peripherals.
  • Compare MCU and MPU-style designs using practical constraints.
  • Explain why an ESP32-class device is a useful IoT teaching platform without treating it as universal.

Start with the system, then choose the compute platform

A microcontroller (MCU) typically integrates a processor core, on-chip memory, and peripheral interfaces intended for direct control of a device. It is designed to read pins, respond to events, run firmware, and often operate with relatively modest power and cost. Many MCUs can boot directly from internal flash and work without a full desktop-style operating system.

A microprocessor (MPU) often emphasizes application-class computation and uses external RAM and storage. Such systems may run Linux or another rich operating system, support processes and filesystems, and host more complex networking, interfaces, or multimedia workloads. They often need a larger power and hardware budget, although real product boundaries vary.

A system-on-chip (SoC) integrates multiple system functions on a chip. Depending on the product, it may contain one or more CPU cores, memory controllers, radio blocks, accelerators, and peripheral controllers. The term describes integration, not a single software model: SoCs can be found in tiny connected devices and powerful application processors.

What belongs in the comparison?

QuestionWhy it matters
What work must run?Simple control, sensing, audio, graphics, signal processing, or a rich user interface lead to different needs.
What timing is required?Some tasks need bounded, predictable response; others can tolerate more scheduling variation.
How much RAM and storage are needed?Buffers, network stacks, filesystems, models, and user interfaces consume different amounts of memory.
What power and thermal limits exist?Battery-operated devices have different constraints from wall-powered gateways.
What interfaces are required?GPIO, ADC, I²C, SPI, UART, USB, Ethernet, Wi-Fi, BLE, and display interfaces influence board choice.
How will the product be maintained?Toolchain support, debugging, security updates, production availability, and vendor longevity matter.

Why we use an ESP32-S3 path

An ESP32-S3 development board is a practical primary platform for this course because it combines microcontroller-style peripheral work with Wi-Fi/Bluetooth Low Energy connectivity and support for ESP-IDF and FreeRTOS. That lets the curriculum progress from GPIO to real networked firmware without changing platforms at every step.

It is not the best choice for every product. A low-power sensor may benefit from a different MCU or radio, a safety-certified control product may require a different architecture, and a rich Linux user interface may need an application processor. The lessons will therefore distinguish general concepts from ESP32-specific APIs and pin behavior.

Choose based on constraints

For a battery sensor that wakes briefly, reads one sensor, and publishes a small message, power modes and radio duty cycle may dominate. For an audio product with streaming, digital signal processing, network communication, and a user interface, processing capacity, memory bandwidth, audio peripherals, latency, and software integration become more important. “Use the most powerful chip” is not a substitute for this comparison.

Practical Lab

Compare two candidate platforms for your example product: an MCU-class board such as ESP32-S3 and an application-processor board that can run Linux. Score each against processing, RAM, power, peripheral support, software complexity, cost, and update/diagnostic needs. Explain what evidence would change your choice.

Expected result: A comparison table that justifies a platform choice against workload, memory, power, connectivity, cost, and development constraints.

Troubleshooting: Check the exact board and chip documentation; avoid assuming every product in an MCU/MPU category has identical capabilities.

Common Pitfall

Assuming MCU, MPU, and SoC are mutually exclusive marketing categories. The useful questions are what functions are integrated, what software runs, and which product constraints the platform satisfies.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why can an MCU be a better choice than a more powerful processor?

A microcontroller may meet performance needs with lower power, simpler boot behavior, integrated peripherals, and lower system complexity.

2. Does the word SoC tell you whether a chip runs Linux?

No. SoC describes integration. The capabilities, memory architecture, vendor software, and product configuration determine the software model.

3. Why does this course use ESP32-S3 without claiming it is best for every project?

It provides one coherent practical path across GPIO, RTOS, Wi-Fi/BLE, and IoT, while platform-selection decisions remain requirements-driven.
